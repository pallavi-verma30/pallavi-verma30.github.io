There's a small bot that applies to jobs for me now. It's the least interesting
part of this story.

It started the way every job hunt starts: too many openings, not enough hours.
So I did the obvious thing first. I pasted a job description into an AI chatbot
and asked it to tailor my resume to the role.

It worked too well.

To match the posting, it nudged my experience up a notch. Smoothed the edges.
Made me sound a bit more senior, a bit more certain. Nothing it wrote was a lie,
exactly. It was just a confident, fluent version of me that, even if I got
shortlisted, I couldn't back up in a room.

The chatbot wasn't broken. It was being obedient. I'd asked it to get me past
the filter, so it optimised for getting past the filter. It had no idea I'd be
sitting across from a person a week later, owning every word. It solved the
problem I typed. It never saw the problem underneath it.

I could have fixed that with a better prompt. Spell out the rules, tell it to
stay grounded, and it behaves. But then I'd be running that prompt by hand for
every single opening, reading every draft, catching every slip, forever. That's
the kind of discipline you run out of by Tuesday. And to be honest, I'm a little
lazy. Building a machine to be diligent for me sounded a lot more appealing than
becoming a diligent person. 😛

So I built the bot. It finds the openings, checks each one against my
background, and only bothers tailoring a resume where I'm genuinely a fit; the
rest it skips. The honesty lives in the pipeline now, not in my willpower, so a
claim can't drift far from something I've actually done. Then it sends me the
ones worth applying to. (Getting it to reliably reach the jobs at all was its
own fight; the modern web is walled off against bots, so I had to redesign it
more than once. A story for another day.)

And it worked. The resumes came out true. They also came out sounding like
everyone else's.

Same fluent, weightless voice all these tools have. "Data-driven insights."
"Operational excellence." "Cross-functional collaboration." I'd fixed the lying
and walked straight into the cliché. Which matters, because the recruiters
reading these can spot that voice from across the room. They see a hundred a
week. A truthful resume that reads "robot written" still loses.

That's the strange part. My application gets written by a machine and then read
by one, and somewhere in the handoff the actual person is supposed to survive.
Mostly they don't. The system loses at both ends. Recruiters, buried under
near-identical applications, can't find the right person in the time they have.
And people who would genuinely fit get dropped over things as small as a missing
keyword, a weak line of positioning, a KRA framed a little too broadly.

It's a real problem. It's also not one you fix by being angry at it. AI was
supposed to make us sharper and take the boring weight off our hands, not bury
us deeper in the data, the platforms, the noise. The tools most of us actually
have, the public ones, not whatever the big companies run behind closed doors,
have made this part harder, not easier.

So the skill worth having now isn't a cleverer prompt. It's adaptability: the
willingness to keep changing your approach as the walls move, which, building
this thing, I did more times than I'd care to admit. The bot didn't make me a
better candidate. It made me a faster learner.

I still use it. I also still read every line before anything goes out, because
the one thing this taught me is that the moment you stop watching is the moment
it starts solving the wrong problem, beautifully.
